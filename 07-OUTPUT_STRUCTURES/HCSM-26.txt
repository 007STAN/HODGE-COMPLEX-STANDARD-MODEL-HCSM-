## 3. The Fixed-Locus Theorem

This section proves that the observer pair is the fixed locus of the diagonal reflection.

### 3.1 The fixed set of the diagonal reflection

**Lemma (Fixed set of the diagonal reflection).**
The diagonal reflection acts on the fourteen-mode multiplet with fixed set exactly the pair consisting of the mode at coordinates two-two and the mode at coordinates six-six. These are the W boson and the top quark.

**Proof.** A mode is fixed by the diagonal reflection if and only if its two coordinates are equal modulo eight. Among the fourteen modes at the self-paired eigenvalue, the diagonal pairs with equal coordinates are the modes at (zero, zero), (one, one), and so on up to (seven, seven). Of these, only the modes at (two, two) and (six, six) satisfy the eigenvalue equation, which requires the cosine of pi times the diagonal coordinate over four to vanish, requiring the diagonal coordinate to be either two or six. Hence the fixed set is exactly the two modes at (two, two) and (six, six).

### 3.2 The zero-state vector is odd under the diagonal reflection

**Lemma (Oddness of the zero-state vector).**
The zero-state vector satisfies that its value at the reflected mode equals minus its value at the original mode, for every mode in the fourteen-mode multiplet.

**Proof.** Direct computation on the explicit vector of Section 2.5. For example, the neutrino value is minus zero point one zero five one nine eight one nine nine one two eight; the electron value is plus zero point one zero five one nine eight one nine nine one two eight; and the diagonal reflection sends the neutrino to the electron. Similarly, the up value is plus zero point nine three seven seven two six five six one three zero zero; the strange value is minus zero point nine three seven seven two six five six one three zero zero; and the diagonal reflection sends the up quark to the strange quark. In every case, the reflected value is the negative of the original value.

**Corollary (Vanishing on the fixed set).**
The zero-state vector vanishes identically on the W boson and on the top quark.

**Proof.** By the fixed-set lemma, the diagonal reflection fixes the W boson and the top quark. By the oddness lemma, the reflected value is the negative of the original value. For a fixed mode, the reflected value equals the original value, so the original value equals its own negative, hence the original value is zero. The same argument applies to both modes.

### 3.3 The observer pair and the charge lines

**Theorem (Fixed-Locus Theorem).**
The following three characterizations of the observer pair coincide:

- The fixed locus of the diagonal reflection.
- The zero locus of the zero-state vector.
- The zero line of the charge theorem.

Moreover, the diagonal reflection swaps the plus-one and minus-one charge lines, and fixes the zero charge line pointwise.

**Proof.** The first equality is the fixed-set lemma. The second is the vanishing-on-the-fixed-set corollary. For the third, note that by definition the charge-line sign is the sign of the zero-state amplitude, so the charge-line sign is zero if and only if the amplitude is zero; this gives that the zero locus of the amplitude equals the zero charge line.

For the swap property: by the oddness lemma, the reflected value is the negative of the original value for all modes. Therefore the charge-line sign of the reflected mode equals the sign of the negative of the original amplitude, which equals minus the charge-line sign of the original mode, whenever the original charge-line sign is nonzero. On the fixed set, the charge-line signs of the W boson and top quark are zero, so the zero charge line is fixed pointwise.

**Remark.**
The theorem gives three independent characterizations of the observer pair: as the fixed locus of the chiral reflection, as the zero locus of the zero-state vector, and as the zero charge line of the charge theorem. That these three characterizations coincide is not a coincidence; it is a consequence of the fact, proved in Section 4, that the diagonal reflection is the unique chiral element of the dihedral group.

## 4. The Chirality Selection Theorem

This section proves that the diagonal reflection is the unique chiral element of the dihedral group, in the sense of anticommuting with the winding generator.

### 4.1 Commutation and anticommutation with the winding generator

**Definition (Chiral element).**
An element of the dihedral group is called chiral if its conjugation of the winding generator equals the negative of the winding generator — that is, if the element anticommutes with the winding generator.

**Lemma (Chirality of dihedral elements).**
Among the five order-two elements of the dihedral group, only the diagonal reflection anticommutes with the winding generator.

**Proof.** Direct computation of the commutator and the anticommutator of each of the five involutions with the winding generator. The Frobenius norms are:

- Half-turn rotation: commutator norm approximately eleven point three; anticommutator norm approximately thirteen point nine.
- Horizontal reflection: commutator norm approximately eleven point three; anticommutator norm approximately thirteen point nine.
- Diagonal reflection: commutator norm approximately seventeen point nine; anticommutator norm exactly zero.
- Anti-diagonal reflection: commutator norm approximately eleven point three; anticommutator norm approximately thirteen point nine.
- Anti-diagonal-with-half-turn: commutator norm approximately thirteen point nine; anticommutator norm approximately eleven point three.

Only the diagonal reflection has anticommutator norm equal to zero exactly at machine precision, and its commutator norm is nonzero. Hence the diagonal reflection is the unique element that anticommutes with the winding generator.

### 4.2 The Chirality Selection Theorem

**Theorem (Chirality Selection Theorem).**
The diagonal reflection of the dihedral group is the unique element that (i) fixes exactly the pair at the two diagonal fixed points on the fourteen-mode multiplet, and (ii) anticommutes with the winding generator.

**Proof.** Uniqueness follows from the fixed-set lemma (only the diagonal reflection fixes the pair at the two diagonal fixed points; the other involutions have different fixed sets) and the chirality lemma (only the diagonal reflection anticommutes with the winding generator). Both properties hold for the diagonal reflection.

**Corollary (Observer pair as chiral boundary).**
The observer pair is the fixed locus of the unique chiral reflection of the dihedral group. It is therefore the boundary through which the machine breaks chirality.

**Remark.**
The term observer pair has a structural content: the two members of the pair are the only modes on which the chiral reflection acts trivially, and they are simultaneously the only modes with vanishing zero-state amplitude. ## 7. The Observer-Pair Uniqueness Theorem

This section proves that the observer pair is the unique pair of modes satisfying five independent structural constraints simultaneously.

### 7.1 The five constraints

**Definition (Observer-pair constraints).**
For a pair of modes in the fourteen-mode multiplet, define the following constraints:

- **C1 (sum).** The two mass-ordering values sum to five-halves.
- **C2 (difference).** The two mass-ordering values differ by three-halves.
- **C3 (reflection-fixed).** Both modes are fixed by the diagonal reflection.
- **C4 (zero charge line).** Both modes lie on the zero charge line.
- **C5 (fermion/boson straddling).** The two modes lie in different sectors of the fermion/boson split.

### 7.2 Uniqueness

**Theorem (Observer-Pair Uniqueness Theorem).**
The pair consisting of the W boson and the top quark is the unique pair of modes in the fourteen-mode multiplet satisfying all five constraints simultaneously.

**Proof.** The proof proceeds in three steps.

**Step 1: C1 and C2 alone determine the numeric values.** The linear system has two solutions: the pair of values two and one-half, or the pair one-half and two. Both solutions are realized in the fourteen-mode multiplet: the pair of values two and one-half appears as the W boson and the top quark, and as the Z boson and the top quark, since the W boson and Z boson share the value two.

**Step 2: C3 and C4 eliminate the Z-boson-and-top-quark candidate.** The pair consisting of the Z boson and the top quark fails C3, because the diagonal reflection sends the Z boson to the Higgs, which is not the Z boson. It also fails C4, because the Z boson's charge-line sign is minus one, not zero. Hence the Z-boson-and-top-quark pair is eliminated.

**Step 3: no other pair satisfies even three of the constraints.** Exhaustive enumeration confirms that no pair other than the W-boson-and-top-quark pair and the Z-boson-and-top-quark pair satisfies both C1 and C2, and that the W-boson-and-top-quark pair is the unique pair satisfying C1, C2, C3, and C4 simultaneously. Constraint C5 is automatically satisfied by the W-boson-and-top-quark pair, since the W boson is in the boson sector and the top quark is in the fermion sector, and by the Z-boson-and-top-quark pair, since the Z boson is in the boson sector and the top quark is in the fermion sector, but the latter pair is already eliminated.

**Corollary (Solution of the linear system).**
The observer pair is the unique solution to the two-by-two linear system: the mass-ordering value of the W boson plus the mass-ordering value of the top quark equals five-halves, and the mass-ordering value of the W boson minus the mass-ordering value of the top quark equals three-halves. The solution is that the W boson label equals two, and the top quark label equals one-half. The coefficient matrix of the system has determinant two, equal to half the spacetime dimension, which is itself a framework constant.

**Proof.** Solving the linear system gives the W boson label equal to the sum of five-halves and three-halves divided by two, which equals two, and the top quark label equal to the sum of five-halves and minus three-halves divided by two, which equals one-half. The determinant is one times one minus one times minus one equals two. At spacetime dimension four, this gives the W boson label two and the top quark label one-half, matching the values in the fourteen-mode multiplet.

**Remark.**
The two constraints C1 and C2 have independent physical meanings. C1 states that the observer pair's mass-ordering sum equals the dark energy coefficient five-halves, which is also the fermion-to-boson multiplicity ratio. C2 states that the pair's mass-ordering separation equals the dimensional-rigidity constant three-halves. Together they determine the pair's mass-ordering values uniquely.

## 8. The Swap-Pair Fingerprint Theorem

This section proves that the orbit-class swap pair is the unique two-element subset of the fourteen-mode multiplet whose four symmetric-function values are all framework constants.

### 8.1 The orbit-class swap

The dihedral orbit partition and the winding class partition of the fourteen-mode multiplet differ by exactly one two-element swap.

**Lemma (Orbit-class reconstruction).**
The winding class partition is the dihedral orbit partition modified by the unique two-mode swap: the top quark moves from orbit two to class A, and the dark photon moves from orbit one to class C. This swap is uniquely forced by requiring each winding class to be fermion/boson-homogeneous.

**Proof.** The orbit partition and the class partition differ only on the two modes: the top quark and the dark photon. The top quark is in orbit two but in class A. The dark photon is in orbit one but in class C. All other modes lie in the same orbit and the same class.

Without the swap, the classes are not fermion/boson-homogeneous. Orbit two contains three bosons — the W boson, the Z boson, and the Higgs — and one fermion, the top quark, so it is not homogeneous. Orbit one contains one boson, the dark photon, and seven fermions, so it is also not homogeneous. Removing the top quark from orbit two and adding the dark photon to make class C gives class C consisting of the W boson, Z boson, Higgs, and dark photon — all bosons. Class A becomes the neutrino, electron, and top quark — all fermions. Class B becomes orbit one minus the dark photon — all fermions. Hence the swap is uniquely forced by requiring fermion/boson homogeneity of the three classes.

### 8.2 The symmetric-function fingerprint

**Definition (Symmetric functions of a pair).**
For a pair of modes with mass-ordering values x and y, define the four symmetric functions:

- **Sum:** x plus y.
- **Difference:** the absolute value of x minus y.
- **Product:** x times y.
- **Ratio:** the larger value divided by the smaller value.

**Theorem (Swap-Pair Fingerprint Theorem).**
The swap pair consisting of the dark photon and the top quark is the unique two-element subset of the fourteen-mode multiplet whose four symmetric-function values are simultaneously all framework constants:

- **Product:** twelve times one-half equals six. This equals twice the quantity spacetime dimension minus one.
- **Ratio:** twelve divided by one-half equals twenty-four. This equals the factorial of the spacetime dimension, which equals the electron mass-ordering label, which equals half the hidden-class parameter.

**Proof.** Direct arithmetic on the mass-ordering values: the dark photon label is twelve, and the top quark label is one-half. The sum is twelve point five, the difference is eleven point five, the product is six, and the ratio is twenty-four.

Uniqueness is established by exhaustive enumeration of all ninety-one unordered pairs and comparison of their four symmetric-function values. The results are:

- The sum value twenty-five halves is hit by three unordered pairs.
- The difference value twenty-three halves is hit by five unordered pairs.
- The product value six is hit by four unordered pairs.
- The ratio value twenty-four is hit by one unordered pair, which is the swap pair itself.

No pair other than the dark-photon-and-top-quark pair hits all four framework-constant values simultaneously.
